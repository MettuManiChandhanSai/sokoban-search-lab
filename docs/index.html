Solver

Pick an algorithm and press Solve. The answer is added to the timeline below.

    How this works
    The four buttons
    Choose who searches. BFS tries everything in order. A* uses a distance estimate to head for the goals. A* + DL also refuses pushes that deadlock rules prove hopeless. A* + DL + M uses a sharper estimate that gives every crate its own goal.
    Solve from here
    Searches from the position on the floor right now, not from the start, so it works after you have made moves. The solution is added to the timeline and played.
    Positions expanded
    How many positions the algorithm took out and examined. These are its search steps, the fairest measure of its work.
    Generated, waiting
    Generated counts every new position it discovered. Waiting is how many of those are still queued.
    Pushes deep
    How many pushes from the start the position under examination is.
    Discarded as hopeless
    Pushes a deadlock rule threw away before they were ever searched.
    The list below the button
    Each algorithm you try on this level is added, so you can compare their search steps and the solution each one found.

    The small board
    The position that algorithm is looking at this instant. BFS jumps all over the room; the informed searches stay near the goals.
    The bar
    Positions expanded so far: the algorithm's search steps. The scale is logarithmic, so a bar one tick longer did ten times the work.

    Every search gets the same budget. An algorithm that runs out gives up, and its count is then a minimum.
    The three graphs

    Same pushes for everyone?
    Yes, by design. All four are guaranteed to find the fewest pushes. They differ in how much they search to get there, and in how far the keeper walks, because several solutions can share the same push count.

    The game

    Sokoban is Japanese for warehouse keeper. You walk the keeper around a storeroom and push crates onto the marked goals. A level is solved when every crate stands on a goal.

    Two rules make it hard. You push one crate at a time, and you can never pull. A crate shoved into a corner stays there for good, so one careless push can make a level impossible.

    How a computer solves it

    The solver thinks in positions: one arrangement of the crates, plus the part of the floor the keeper can walk to. From a position it can make a push, which leads to a new position. Solving means finding a chain of pushes from the start to a position where every crate is home.

    The number of positions explodes. Three crates in a small room already allow tens of thousands. The four algorithms differ only in the order they look through them, and in what they refuse to look at.

    BFS (breadth-first search)
    Looks at every position one push from the start, then every position two pushes away, and so on. It has no idea which direction is promising. It is the baseline the others are measured against.
    A*
    Adds a heuristic, an estimate of how many pushes are still needed: for each crate, the grid distance to its nearest goal. A* always examines the position where pushes made plus pushes estimated is smallest, so it heads for the goals.
    A* + Deadlock
    Adds knowledge. Before a push is even considered, four rules check whether it has just lost the game. If so it is thrown away, along with everything that would have followed.
    A* + Deadlock + Matching
    Adds a sharper heuristic. Plain distance sees through walls and lets every crate aim at the same goal. This one measures real push distance and gives each crate a goal of its own, choosing the cheapest pairing.

    Why all four report the same number of pushes

    Because all four are guaranteed to find a solution with the fewest pushes possible. That is the point of the comparison: the smarter algorithms do not cut corners. They reach the same best answer after examining a small fraction of the positions.

    What does differ:

    Search steps
    Positions expanded. On a hard level BFS examines tens of thousands where the best algorithm examines a few hundred.
    Time
    Follows from the search steps.
    Keeper moves
    Many different solutions can share the same push count while the keeper walks a different route. Each algorithm stops at the first optimal solution it meets, so their move counts often differ.

    The differences are small on the training levels and large on the Showdown pack, which was chosen for exactly this.

    Reading the numbers

    Positions expanded
    Positions the algorithm took out and examined. Its search steps.
    Generated
    New positions it discovered while doing so.
    Waiting
    Discovered positions not yet examined (the frontier).
    Pushes deep
    How far from the start the search currently is.
    Pruned, discarded
    Pushes rejected by a deadlock rule before being searched.
    Pushes, moves
    The length of a solution, counted in crate pushes and in keeper steps.
    Out of budget, out of time
    Every search has the same limit. One that hits it gives up, and its numbers are minimums.

    The deadlock rules

    A deadlock is a position that can never be solved, whatever you do next. Turn on Dead squares in Play to see the first three on any level.

    Corner trap
    A crate with a wall on one side and another wall above or below can never be pushed again.
    Wall-line trap
    A crate against a wall can only slide along it. If that stretch of wall has no goal, the crate is lost.
    Dead square
    The general case: a square from which no sequence of pushes reaches any goal.
    Freeze deadlock
    Crates that block each other so that none can move, with at least one of them off its goal. Two crates side by side against a wall is the classic.

    In Play, the moment you cause one of these a warning names the rule, so you can undo straight away.

    Under the hood

    The rules, the four algorithms, the level generator and the benchmark are written in Python. This page loads those same Python files and runs them in your browser, so the numbers you see here are the numbers the command-line tools print.
